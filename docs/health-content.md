## Heart rate
- **Unit:** bpm
- **Body system:** Cardiovascular
- **Normal range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see notes)
- **Caution range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see notes)
- **How to measure:** Sit or rest quietly for 5 minutes. Use the pulse sensor or count beats at the wrist for 30 seconds and multiply by 2. Measure at the same time each day.
- **Why it matters in space:** Fluids shift toward the head in weightlessness, and the body adjusts how it controls the heart. NASA found heart rate and its daily ups and downs are lower in flight. A big jump from your own baseline can be an early sign of stress, illness, or deconditioning.
- **Related hazard:** Cardiovascular deconditioning; fast heart rate (tachycardia) after returning to gravity.
- **Source:** https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040201532 ; https://core.ac.uk/works/15260545
- **Notes / flags:** NASA doesn't give a fixed resting range. Team needs to decide: (a) use a general medical source like the American Heart Association for the fixed range, labeled as non-NASA, or (b) rely only on change from the astronaut's baseline.

## Systolic blood pressure
- **Unit:** mmHg
- **Body system:** Cardiovascular
- **Normal range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- **Caution range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- **How to measure:** Sit still for 5 minutes with your arm supported at heart level. Put the cuff on your bare upper arm and stay still and quiet while it reads. Measure at the same time each day.
- **Why it matters in space:** A NASA-funded study of astronauts on 6-month missions found 24-hour systolic pressure dropped in space (about 120 mmHg before flight to about 106 mmHg in flight) and went back to normal after landing. The bigger risk is returning to gravity: astronauts can have trouble keeping their blood pressure up when they stand.
- **Related hazard:** Orthostatic intolerance (dizziness or fainting when standing) after returning to gravity, for example on Mars.
- **Source:** Fu et al., Circulation 2019, Impact of Prolonged Spaceflight on Orthostatic Tolerance and BP Profiles: https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.119.041050
NASA HRP Evidence Report, Risk of Orthostatic Intolerance During Re-Exposure to Gravity: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf
- **Notes / flags:** No fixed NASA range. The in-flight numbers are group averages from a study, not safety limits.

## Diastolic blood pressure
- Unit: mmHg
- Body system: Cardiovascular
- Normal range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- Caution range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- How to measure: Same reading as systolic. The cuff gives both numbers (systolic is the   top number, diastolic is the bottom one).
- Why it matters in space: Studies differ. The Shuttle-era NASA study found diastolic pressure was lower in flight, while the 6-month ISS study found it unchanged. Either way, a change from your own baseline is the thing to watch.
- Related hazard: Cardiovascular deconditioning; orthostatic intolerance after returning to gravity.
- Warning signs: Same as systolic.
- Source:
NASA, Cardiovascular Deconditioning (EDOMP): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040201532
Fu et al., Circulation 2019: https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.119.041050
Notes / flags: No fixed NASA range.

## SpO2 (blood oxygen)
- **Unit:** %
- **Body system:** Respiratory
- **Normal range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see notes)
- **Caution range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see notes)
- **How to measure:** Sit still and clip the pulse oximeter on a fingertip. Keep your hand still and warm. Wait until the number stops changing (about 30 seconds), then record it.
- **Why it matters in space:** Oxygen keeps your body's tissues working. Low oxygen (hypoxia) can affect mood, senses, thinking and coordination. It's easy to miss, because the symptoms can feel like normal tiredness, and it can make it harder to notice you have a problem.
- **Related hazard:** Hypoxia from a cabin atmosphere or life-support problem.
- **Warning signs:** Tiredness, nausea, headache, dizziness, mood changes.
- **Source:** NASA OCHMO-TB-003 Habitable Atmosphere: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-003-habitable-atmosphere.pdf
- **Notes / flags:** NASA's brief gives no SpO2 % range. Same team decision as heart rate.

## Body temperature
- **Unit:** °C
- Body system: (team decides, see team decision 4)
- Normal range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decisions 1 and 2)
- Caution range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decisions 1 and 2)
- How to measure: Rest for 10 minutes first, and don't measure right after exercise. Use the forehead or oral thermometer the same way and at the same time each day.
- Why it matters in space: A study of astronauts on the ISS (with NASA Johnson Space Center co-authors) found resting core body temperature slowly rose by about 1 °C over 2.5 months in space, to around 38 °C. During exercise it rose higher and faster than on Earth, sometimes above 40 °C. Even small increases can hurt physical and mental performance.
- Related hazard: Heat buildup in weightlessness (no natural airflow to carry heat away); infection.
- Warning signs: Temperature well above your own in-flight baseline, feeling overheated, unusual tiredness.
- Source:
Stahn et al., Scientific Reports 2017, Increased core body temperature in astronauts during long-duration space missions: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-15560-w
- Notes / flags: No fixed range. Earth fever cutoffs would wrongly flag healthy astronauts, so compare to each astronaut's baseline.

## Sleep duration
- Unit: hours
- Body system: Behavioral health
- Normal range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- Caution range: NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- How to measure: When you wake up, enter about how many hours you actually slept (not just time in bed). If you wear a sleep tracker, use its number.
- Why it matters in space: NASA studies show astronauts often sleep less in space than on the ground, around 6 to 6.5 hours a night on the Shuttle and ISS. Sleep studies cited by NASA found that after 7 days of 6 hours of sleep, a person performs about as badly as someone who has been awake for 24 to 36 hours straight.
- Related hazard: Fatigue and performance errors from sleep loss, circadian disruption (no normal day/night cycle) and work overload.
- Warning signs: Several short nights in a row, trouble focusing, slower reactions, mood changes.
- Source:
NASA OCHMO-TB-041 Sleep Accommodations: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/sleep-accomodations-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf
NASA, Astronaut Sleep Duration Varies by Timing of Scheduled Sleep: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230003916
- Notes / flags: No NASA target range for a check-in app. Sleep loss over several nights in a row is a good fit for a trend-based alert.

## Mood
- **Unit:** scale (see team decision 3)
- **Body system:** Behavioral health
- **Normal range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- **Caution range:** NO NASA SOURCE FOUND (see team decision 1)
- **How to measure:** Once a day, at the same time, pick the number that best matches how you've felt overall today.
- **Why it matters in space:** Isolation, confinement, distance from Earth and stress can affect mood, morale, thinking and teamwork. NASA notes astronauts can be reluctant to report these problems, and 2 of 7 NASA Shuttle-Mir astronauts reported depressive symptoms. NASA is working on semi-autonomous behavioral health monitoring for future missions, which is what this app's daily check-in does in a simple way.
- **Related hazard:** Adverse cognitive or behavioral conditions from isolation and confinement.
- **Source:** NASA OCHMO-TB-016 Behavioral Health and Performance: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf
NASA OCHMO-TB-014 Behavioral Health Mishaps: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-014-behavioral-health-mishaps.pdf
NASA HRP, Human Factors and Behavioral Performance overview (semi-autonomous monitoring goal): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002656/downloads/HRP%20IWS%202026%20HFBP%20Overview%20export%20control%20042026.pdf
- **Notes / flags:** No NASA scale or range. Guidance for low mood should point to talking with the crew medical officer and a private conference with the ground psychology team, not to self-diagnosis.
